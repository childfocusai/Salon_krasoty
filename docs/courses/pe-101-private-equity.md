## Introducing Private Equity

*Published 2026-04-12. Source: https://ilyastrebulaev.substack.com/p/introducing-private-equity*

Private equity powers everything from early-stage startups to billion-dollar buyouts, yet its mechanics remain opaque to most people touched by it. Whether you're a founder raising venture capital, a VC deploying it, or someone whose pension fund has a significant PE allocation, understanding how PE actually works is practical, not academic.

Founders negotiating term sheets benefit from understanding the structural incentives of the funds writing those checks — why a VC might push for a follow-on round, how GP commitment shapes risk appetite, or what a fund's lifecycle means for exit timing pressure. For VCs and aspiring fund managers, the legal architecture, compensation mechanics, and LP dynamics covered here are the operating system of the business. And for the general public, the companies we work for, the services we use, and the retirement assets we depend on are all shaped by the capital flows and governance structures described in these pages. What follows is an overview of those foundations.

> **What is Private Equity**

Private Equity (PE) typically refers to investments in privately held equity or equity-like securities. The critical qualifier is “privately held,” meaning that these investments are not actively traded on the stock market.

The PE goal is to select and make these investments, enhance their value over time, and ultimately exit at a profit. PE investments cover a wide gamut of assets. They include buyout and growth investments in mature companies, as well as venture capital investments in early-stage companies. PE investments also cover real estate, infrastructure, and other PE funds and firms. Although “equity” in “private equity” implies that most investments are in equity-like securities and “private” is in privately held deals, “PE” also may include private credit investments as well as what is known as private investment in public equity (PIPE). Especially in recent years PE is becoming more multifaceted, and so while I will try my best to avoid using the words “typically” or “mostly” or “often” in describing PE practices and conventions, you always need to keep in mind that these words could easily populate half of the sentences in my text.

Two most important PE investment types are buyouts and venture capital (VC). They are quite different in terms of investments they make (investments are known as portfolio companies). Buyout funds typically acquire majority ownership in their investments, meaning that they can exercise a large degree of control. VC funds typically acquire a minority position. Buyout funds often use leverage to finance their investments. VC funds rarely use leverage. Buyout funds can make just one-time investment in a portfolio company. VC funds tend to make follow-on investments as their portfolio companies fundraise again and again.

As buyout funds invest in more mature companies and VC funds invest in early-stage companies, it makes sense to conduct any performance analysis of buyout and VC funds separately, though comparisons between them are often quite illuminating.

Many aspects of economics of buyout and VC funds are, however, similar and follow the same patterns and mechanisms. A lot of what I cover applies to buyout, VC, and other corners of the PE industry. This covers legal arrangements, compensation structures, and relationship between fund managers and their investors.

*Caveat: The term PE may refer to the broad understanding of PE (that covers all investments described above, including VC) and the narrow understanding of PE that covers buyouts only. I will use “PE” in a broad context.*

> **Structure of PE Firms and Funds**

In a conversation, PE firms and PE funds are often interchanged, but there is an important distinction between them. A PE firm organizes multiple funds over time and often manages more than one fund at a time. For example, Blackstone is a PE firm, Blackstone IX is a PE fund managed by Blackstone, and Blackstone Opportunity Fund X is another PE fund managed by Blackstone.

Legally, each PE fund is typically set up as a distinct entity and structured as a limited partnership between limited partners (LPs) and a general partner (GP). Each new fund is typically formed and raised separately from its predecessors. Even though legally the situation is more complicated, from the economic point of view the PE firm that organizes the fund also is behind the GP of that fund.

LPs are the investors who provide most of the fund’s investment capital. Many of them are institutional investors, such as pension funds (e.g., CalPERS), endowments (e.g., Stanford Management Company), and sovereign wealth funds (e.g., Abu Dhabi Investment Authority). Family offices also increasingly invest in PE funds. The goal of LPs is to allocate capital among many opportunities to generate attractive investment returns. One PE fund usually has many LPs.

The GP is a fiduciary responsible for managing and controlling the fund on behalf of the LPs. This includes making effectively all investment decisions. Economically, the GP is ultimately owned by one or more partners of the PE firm. Legally, the GP is typically organized as a limited liability company or limited partnership. This structure separates the GP from both the PE firm and the fund itself. One advantage it brings is to limit the personal liability of PE firm partners.

The GP typically hires an investment manager or management company to make investment decisions and operate the fund on its behalf, though the GP retains a fiduciary duty to the fund and its investors. A PE firm may either act as a management company itself or form a separate management company to provide operating services to the GP. The management company hires employees who do the day-to-day work. When I refer to “PE firm” in terms of its activities, I cover collectively the GP and the management company. They involve the same people, though the economic relationships between these people could be very complex. Often, a subset of PE firm partners holds a substantial ownership stake in the management company, though its ownership may differ from that of the PE firm itself.

An important aspect of the separation between the GP and the management company is that the income stream to PE firms comes in two main distinct ways. Management companies get management fee for managing the fund. The GP gets the profit share from the fund returns.

> **Contractual Relationships in the PE Industry**

All the entities that are involved in a PE fund maintain their relationships with each other through various agreements, including the Limited Partnership Agreement (LPA), Operating Agreement, and Management Agreement. All these legal agreements are custom tailored and often heavily negotiated. The fund agreements cover all LPs, although LPs may negotiate some additional provisions.

The broad economic context of these agreements is twofold. First, the agreements prescribe how future cash flows will be managed and shared. Second, they impose various restrictions that constrain parties in their future actions.

Here are the main agreements that any LP can expect to see.

- **Limited Partnership Agreement (LPA):** The LPA governs the fund structure and the relationship between the GP and LPs. For a particular fund, the LPA outlines the investment period, the fund’s duration, the capital commitment from LPs, the capital contributions from the GP, the compensation of the GP, and the lifespan of the limited partnership.
- **Private Placement Memorandums (PPMs):** PPMs tell LPs what they are getting into. This includes fund organizational details, investment strategy, risks, and legal considerations.
- **Subscription Agreements (SA):** SA contains the terms of LP capital commitments and securities law exemptions.
- **Investment Management Agreement:** The Management Agreement governs the relationship between the GP and the management company. In particular, it describes the management fees that the management company receives.
- **Side letters:** Side letters contain LP-specific contractual provisions and exemptions. While the documents above are the same for all LPs, side letters are negotiated between PE fund managers and each LP individually. They are becoming more widespread.
  Another important legal document covers the relationship on the PE firm side.
- **Operating Agreement:** An Operating Agreement outlines the governance structure of the GP. It includes the GP ownership structure, management rules, voting rights, and the allocation of profit.

> **LPs Do Not Actively Participate in PE Fund’s Life**

In a typical PE fund, LPs are passive investors. They do not participate in day-to-day management and do not make specific investment decisions.

LPs may exercise formal control over some of fund’s important decisions, the specifics of which depend on the LPA. Most PE funds have LP advisory committee (LPAC). LPAC often consists of the representatives of large LPs. LPAC has the right to approve certain decisions by the GP, such as making investing decisions representing a conflict of interest. For example, LPACs often have the right to approve an investment in a portfolio company that was invested earlier by another PE fund of the same PE firm. For funds with a limited lifespan, LPs also have the right to approve extension of fund’s life. One of the most important LPA provisions is the so-called key-man provision that is triggered when specific fund managers are not able to fulfil their duties. Upon the key-man trigger, LPs typically acquire additional rights, which can include the right to dissolve the PE fund partnership.

LPs do make very important decisions, just not at a level of individual portfolio companies. Their decisions include fund manager selection and allocation, including a decision to invest in follow-on funds by the same fund manager.

> **Committed Capital**

When a PE fund starts, LPs promise to contribute a certain amount of capital over a specific period of time. This is called “committed capital.” An important distinction between PE funds and many other investments, such as mutual or hedge funds, is that LPs commit to contribute capital but they do not provide this capital at the outset of the fund. Rather, the GP asks LPs to send the money from time to time in fulfilment of their commitment. This is known as capital calls.

This arrangement dramatically changes the economics of PE funds. If you commit to invest $100 million in a mutual fund, you wire $100 million right away. If you commit to invest $100 million to a PE fund, the GP may be asking you to wire different amounts each quarter over the next several years. This raises a host of questions: should you reserve the entire $100 million in anticipation of the capital calls? How should you invest these $100 million or whatever is left before all of them is sent to the fund manager? All these questions have an impact on investor decisions, their risks, and PE performance metrics.

> **GP Commitment**

Like in other contractual relationships, incentives problems and conflicts of interest are common in PE. If anything, these issues are more acute because PE funds invest in privately held assets about which less is known. There are many ways investors try to attenuate conflicts and align interests of fund managers and LPs. One way is GP commitment, better known as the skin in the game.

In the GP commitment, fund managers invest their own money alongside LPs. The economic idea is simple. If the fund performs well, both LPs and fund managers will benefit. And if the fund performs poorly, fund managers will lose money alongside LPs. Research shows that “skin in the game” has an impact on investment performance. For example, for VC funds, higher GP contribution is linked to superior fund performance.¹

Many people in the industry think that fund managers typically collectively contribute one percent of total capital. The figure below, from a recent academic paper by Greg Brown and William Volckman, shows the distribution of GP commitment using data from 1,503 PE funds run by 917 PE firms from 2000–2019 vintages.² It is clear that GP commitment is often larger than 1%. There is also a large degree of variation. There are many PE funds with 5% and more, and quite a few funds where GP commitment is 10% or above of the total fund size.

> **Vintage Year and Life Cycle of the PE Fund**

Another important concept in PE is “vintage year.” For a PE fund, vintage year is the year when the fund starts investing in portfolio companies. Vintage year is important because most of the performance analysis is done relative to the comparison set consisting of other similar funds and this most often includes vintage cohorts.

Even though the idea of the vintage year seems very simple, there are many nuances. What if the fund closes (meaning it gets all the commitments from LPs and no longer accepts new commitments) in November 2026 and starts investing in 2027. Is it a 2026 or 2027 vintage year? What if the fund closes in several stages (as many first-time funds do). The fund has its first close (when some LPs commit) in November 2026, makes its first investment in March 2026, and has its final close in March 2028. Is it 2026, 2027, or 2028 vintage year? The answer could be important for the performance analysis.

Most PE funds generally have a finite life, even though evergreen funds (i.e., those without a specified horizon that in principle can continue indefinitely) have been seen here and there. The fund lifespan is typically ten years, with the possibility to extend by a few more years. In practice, many funds, especially VC funds, end up having a longer horizon, because it may take a very long time to exit all the investments.

A typical PE fund makes investments in portfolio companies in the first four to six years, a period often known as the “commitment period” or “investment period.” Once this period is over, the PE fund can typically only make follow-on investments in the companies that it has already invested in.
