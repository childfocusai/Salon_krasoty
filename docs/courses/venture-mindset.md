## What the course / book is

Strebulaev has taught venture capital at Stanford GSB for many years and, with Alex Dang, turned the decision-making research into an executive framework: nine principles of how VCs think, aimed at leaders in any industry that now faces high uncertainty.

The claim is not that every decision should be made like a VC. Day-to-day operations, budgeting, and supply-chain work still need a traditional, low-error mindset. The venture mindset is for environments with many unknowns, frequent change, and a few outcomes that dominate all the others.

Strebulaev: the traditional mindset works in a stable environment. The venture mindset works where nobody knows the truth yet and the cost of missing the next outlier is higher than the cost of many failed tries.

Dang: in a stable world you would rather not miss (not fail often). In a venture world you would rather not miss your next Google, so you swing for the fences and accept strikeouts.

## The nine principles

These are the nine chapter titles Strebulaev and Dang use. Explanations below are from the public Stanford interview and talks, not a reprint of the book chapters.

### 1. Home Runs Matter, Strikeouts Don’t

VC returns are power-law, not average-in, average-out. A few investments pay for the fund; most do not. The relevant error is missing the home run, not recording a strikeout.

If “luck” happens again and again, Strebulaev’s answer is that repeated luck is skill.

Dang’s Amazon example: he kept a wall of press releases that included failures, to make the point that a real innovation portfolio must contain them.

### 2. Get Outside the Four Walls

Deal flow and new ideas do not come from sitting inside the same building with the same people. VCs build wide, diverse networks because outliers do not announce themselves inside headquarters.

Strebulaev’s personal version: he used to board a plane and talk to nobody. The principle pushed him to talk to strangers; twenty conversations may yield nothing, the twenty-first may be a research idea, a deal, or a book.

### 3. Prepare Your Mind

A prepared mind can recognize an opportunity that looks like noise to everyone else. In the public talks this is paired with staying current on technology, markets, and people so that when the unusual signal appears, you can act.

### 4. Say No 100 Times

For each investment, a typical VC reviews about 100 companies and says no to 99. The public research number Strebulaev cites is about 101 companies reviewed per investment.

This is the opposite of a stable-world search, where options are similar and extra search has low payoff. In an outlier world you pay the search cost because one option is not “marginally better” — it is orders of magnitude better.

Fast lane vs slow lane (same interview, useful for founders):

- Top of funnel (fast lane): the question is “Why should we *not* proceed?” One critical red flag and the meeting is over.
- Bottom of funnel (slow lane): real diligence, more people in the room, different questions. Founders can tell which lane they are in by who they meet and what they are asked.

### 5. Bet on the Jockey

At early stage, VCs weight the founding / management team (“jockey”) more than the current business plan (“horse”). They still care about market and model — “you cannot win with a donkey” — but the plan will change. The bet is on the people who will solve problems that are not in the deck.

Dang: you can polish projections; in six months reality is different. The team is what you actually bought.

### 6. Agree to Disagree

Consensus is treated as dangerous in this setting. Alex Rampell (a16z), quoted by Strebulaev: “Conviction beats consensus.” A deal can go forward if it has a champion who convinces some partners, not all of them.

Reid Hoffman / Airbnb example from the interview: Hoffman wanted to invest; the partnership was against it; a senior partner said every VC needs a deal they can fail on, and Airbnb could be his. In a traditional organization the investment would not have happened.

### 7. Double Down or Quit

This is staged capital, not “bet the farm.” Start with a small check (seed / Series A). Collect signals from users. Then either write a much larger follow-on or stop.

Dang: this is how the mindset stays distinct from gambling. You do not put the whole fund on one idea on day one.

### 8. Make the Pie Bigger

Public talks frame this as expanding the opportunity — creating new markets and new value — rather than only fighting over a fixed pie. Corporate examples they cite of internal “unicorns”: Nestlé / Nespresso, Tencent / WeChat, Google / Gmail and Google Cloud, Apple / iPhone.

### 9. Great Things Take Time

VC funds are structured around a long horizon (classically about ten years, often longer in practice). The principle is to ignore temporary swings and not kill a compounding bet because the quarter looks bad.

## Why large companies lose this mindset

Strebulaev: successful corporations become risk-averse because they have assets and cash flows to protect. If leadership does not control process and culture on purpose, the traditional mindset is the default, and it is hard to reverse.

Dang: the natural tendency to risk-aversion must not be allowed to spread through the whole organization. Some large firms have still created new ventures inside themselves; it is possible, not automatic.

The venture mindset should not be applied to every decision. Perfection still belongs in operations. It belongs on innovative bets.

## Fast-rejection screen (public Stanford note)

In the fast lane, VCs do not start with “How great is this?” They start with “What would make this a clear no?” Founders who do not know that process often spend the meeting selling upside while the investor is already scanning for a disqualifying flaw.
